Literature search and structured evidence extraction on **AI for Social Good (AI4SG) tools in public service delivery** in **Bangladesh, India, Indonesia and Thailand**.

## **1. Objective**

Identify relevant empirical studies, evaluations, conference papers, working papers, government reports, audit reports, and credible institutional reports on **AI-enabled public-service tools that have been actually tested, deployed, implemented, or used in real public-service settings** in Bangladesh, India, Indonesia, or Thailand.

**Geographic scope:** a source is eligible if the AI tool was tested or used in at least one of the four countries. Multi-country studies are eligible only if results, sample sizes, or implementation details for at least one of the four countries are reported separately; extract only the country-specific information.

The purpose is **not** to assess whether the literature is strong or weak, identify research gaps, or rank papers by causal quality.

The purpose is to extract factual information that can later be used for analysis of:

- intervention

- adoption

- effectiveness

- implementation

- deployment

- scaling

- cost

- financing

Focus on collecting accurate and comparable information across studies.

## **2. Definition of AI4SG in public service**

For this review, define **AI4SG in public service** as:

> The use of AI-enabled systems to address socially important public-service problems by improving citizens' access to, and the quality, timeliness, equity, accountability, or efficiency of, services that are delivered, financed, or mandated by government, especially where citizens, frontline officials, or public agencies face resource, capacity, language, or access constraints.

Services delivered by NGOs, social enterprises, or private firms are eligible when they are delivered under a public mandate, in partnership with a public agency, or as a substitute for a public service (e.g., public agricultural extension, disaster early warning).

Do not require the paper itself to use the term "AI for Social Good."

Include an application when:

1.  AI is a substantive component of the tool or service;

2.  the application addresses a public-service problem; and

3.  the intended outcome concerns access to services or entitlements, service quality, timeliness, equity, inclusion, accountability, frontline capacity, affordability, or public-sector performance.

## **3. AI applications to include**

Include, where relevant:

- citizen-facing chatbots and virtual assistants

- multilingual speech recognition, text-to-speech, and machine translation for public services

- LLM or retrieval-augmented advisory services (e.g., scheme information, agricultural advice, legal information)

- fraud, leakage, and anomaly detection in benefits, subsidies, procurement, or tax

- automated classification and routing of citizen complaints and service requests

- forecasting and early-warning systems (e.g., riverine floods, extreme weather, crop pests) linked to public alerts

- satellite, remote-sensing, or geospatial AI used operationally for service planning, damage assessment, or targeting

- computer vision for infrastructure and municipal-service monitoring

- OCR and NLP for administrative records and document processing

- translation and summarisation of court judgments and legal documents

- decision support for frontline officials and extension workers

- predictive resource allocation for public programmes

Older systems may be included if machine learning, prediction, NLP, automated classification, or automated personalisation are substantive components.

Exclude ordinary digital-government tools where AI is not a substantive component (e.g., online forms, portals, rule-based IVR menus, SMS broadcasts). Keyword-triggered bots without NLP or learning components should be recorded as **borderline** and flagged rather than silently included.

## **4. Type of evidence to prioritize**

Prioritize sources that contain information on **real interventions or real deployments**, including:

- randomized trials

- field experiments

- quasi-experimental evaluations

- implementation studies

- deployment evaluations

- scaling studies

- observational studies using actual usage data or administrative records

- government evaluations and annual programme reports

- audit-institution and parliamentary reports

- algorithmic audits and accountability reports (including civil-society technical audits)

- international-organization reports (e.g., World Bank, United Nations Development Programme, Asian Development Bank, International Telecommunication Union, Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development)

- credible program evaluations

- implementation reports

- cost or financing reports

Do not prioritize:

- opinion pieces

- purely conceptual papers

- general discussions of AI in government

- attitude-only studies

- intention-to-use or technology-acceptance surveys without actual usage data

- purely technical machine-learning papers without real public-service deployment

- papers where AI is used only for generic convenience

### **Sources to search**

Prioritize the sources listed below. They are not the only sources to use. You should also search more widely in any other databases, journals, conference proceedings, working-paper series, repositories, organizational websites, or grey-literature sources you judge likely to contain relevant studies. This includes checking the reference lists and citing articles of relevant studies.

Where possible, connect multiple sources referring to the same AI tool or program.

## **6. Unit of analysis**

Use the **AI intervention or deployment** as the main unit of analysis, rather than the paper alone.

If several papers evaluate the same AI tool in different settings or stages, treat them as separate intervention records but connect them using a common:

- Tool / program name

- Organization / provider

- Program ID if useful

## **7. Information to extract**

For every relevant intervention, extract only information that is directly useful for later analysis.

### **A. Source information**

- Full citation

- Authors

- Year

- Paper/report title

- Journal, conference (and track), or institution

- DOI

- Official URL

- Trial or registry ID, if any

- Publication type

### **B. Context**

- Country

- State / province / district / city, if reported

- Country income level (World Bank classification for the study year)

- Urban / rural / mixed

- Service sector

- Level of government (national; state or province; district; municipal; village or local-government unit)

- Implementing agency type (ministry or department; local government; public enterprise; NGO under public mandate; public-private partnership)

- Target population (e.g., citizens, welfare beneficiaries, farmers, flood-exposed households, frontline officials)

- Number of citizens, households, or beneficiaries

- Number of officials or frontline workers

- Number of agencies or administrative units

- Other relevant sample characteristics (e.g., literacy, smartphone ownership, gender)

### **C. Public-service problem**

Record the main problem the AI tool is intended to address.

Examples:

- low awareness of entitlements and schemes

- complex application procedures

- language and literacy barriers

- long processing times and backlogs

- inclusion and exclusion errors in targeting

- leakage, duplication, or fraud

- shortage of frontline staff (e.g., extension workers)

- slow complaint resolution and weak accountability

- inadequate or late disaster warnings

- limited service reach in remote areas

- high administrative cost

- fragmented administrative data

Use the authors' description where possible.

### **D. AI application**

Extract:

- AI tool / program name and version

- Provider / developer

- AI application category

- Technology type (e.g., LSTM forecasting model, text classifier, LLM with retrieval)

- Main function of the AI

- Decision role of the AI:

  - information only (no decision)

  - recommendation to a human official

  - automated triage or routing

  - automated decision affecting eligibility or entitlement

- Primary user:

  - citizen / beneficiary

  - frontline official or extension worker

  - intermediary (e.g., village volunteer, service-centre operator)

  - administrator or agency manager

  - policymaker

  - other

Application categories may include:

- citizen information / navigation

- eligibility / targeting

- fraud / anomaly detection

- grievance / complaint management

- forecasting / early warning

- advisory services (e.g., agriculture, livelihoods)

- language access / translation

- document and records processing

- decision support for officials

- resource allocation / planning

### **E. Intervention design**

Extract:

- What exactly was introduced?

- Who received the intervention?

- What did users do with the AI?

- What role did officials, frontline workers, or intermediaries play?

- Did AI:

  - replace a human decision

  - recommend a decision to an official

  - triage or route cases

  - provide information or advice directly to citizens

  - make predictions or issue alerts

  - support administration

- Human oversight of AI outputs

- Grievance or appeal mechanism for AI-driven decisions

- Duration

- Frequency

- Intensity

- Location of use

- Control/comparison condition, if applicable

- organizational requirements

### **H. Scaling**

Extract factual information on:

- initial pilot size

- later deployment size

- number of citizens or households reached

- number of queries, transactions, or alerts

- number of agencies or organizations onboarded

- number of officials

- number of local-government units

- number of districts

- number of states / provinces

- geographic expansion

- expansion over time

- whether government adopted the tool

- whether the tool was integrated into routine government operations

- legal or institutional mandate for use

- institutionalization

- procurement

- modifications made when scaling

- staffing changes

- infrastructure changes

- operational requirements at scale

Do not label something "scaled" only because the authors describe it as scalable. Record actual deployment numbers where possible, and distinguish official or developer-reported figures from independently verified figures.

## **8. Missing information**

Do not guess.

If a field is unavailable, record:

**Not reported**

If information was searched for but could not be verified, record:

**Not verified**

Do not infer values from surrounding information.

## **10. Final output requirements**

The main output should be a **clean, verified evidence database** that can later be used for cross-study analysis.

The goal is:

> **To collect comparable factual information on how AI public-service tools in Bangladesh, India, Indonesia, and Thailand are designed, adopted, implemented, deployed, used, scaled, funded, and associated with outcomes for citizens and public agencies, including errors and harms.**
