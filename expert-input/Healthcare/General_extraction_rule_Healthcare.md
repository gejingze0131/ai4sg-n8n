Literature search and structured evidence extraction on **AI for Social Good (AI4SG) tools in the healthcare sector** in **Bangladesh, India, Indonesia and Thailand**.

## **1. Objective**

Identify relevant empirical studies, evaluations, conference papers, working papers, government reports, and credible institutional reports on **AI-enabled health tools that have been actually tested, deployed, implemented, or used in real health-care or public-health settings** in Bangladesh, India, Indonesia, or Thailand.

**Geographic scope:** a source is eligible if the AI tool was tested or used in at least one of the four countries. Multi-country studies are eligible only if results, sample sizes, or implementation details for at least one of the four countries are reported separately; extract only the country-specific information.

The purpose is **not** to assess whether the literature is strong or weak, identify research gaps, or rank papers by causal quality.

The purpose is to extract factual information that can later be used for analysis of:

- intervention

- adoption

- effectiveness (clinical, programme, and health-system effects)

- diagnostic and predictive performance in real settings

- implementation

- deployment

- scaling

- safety and equity

- cost

- financing

Focus on collecting accurate and comparable information across studies.

## **2. Definition of AI4SG in healthcare**

For this review, define **AI4SG in healthcare** as:

> The use of AI-enabled systems to address socially important health problems by improving access to care, the quality and timeliness of screening, diagnosis or treatment, health outcomes, equity, health-workforce capacity, or the efficiency of health-service delivery and public-health programmes, especially where patients, health workers, facilities, or health systems face resource, capacity, or access constraints.

Do not require the paper itself to use the term "AI for Social Good."

Include an application when:

1.  AI is a substantive component of the tool or service;

2.  the application addresses a health or public-health problem; and

3.  the intended outcome concerns access, quality of care, health outcomes, equity, health-worker capacity, affordability, or health-system performance.

## **3. AI applications to include**

Include, where relevant:

- AI-assisted screening and diagnosis from medical images (e.g., chest X-ray computer-aided detection for tuberculosis, retinal images for diabetic retinopathy, breast, cervical, skin, ultrasound)

- point-of-care or offline AI on smartphones and portable devices

- clinical decision support systems (CDSS) for doctors, nurses, or community health workers

- AI triage and referral prioritisation

- autonomous AI diagnostic systems

- LLM or chatbot health information for patients, caregivers, or community health workers

- AI-supported telemedicine and teleconsultation

- risk prediction and early-warning systems (e.g., high-risk pregnancy, treatment non-adherence, disengagement from a health programme)

- AI for targeting outreach and allocating scarce health-worker time

- personalised adherence and behaviour-change messaging driven by AI

- AI-based disease surveillance and outbreak forecasting used operationally

- maternal, newborn, and child-health AI (e.g., anthropometry, newborn weight estimation)

- mental-health conversational agents

- AI speech recognition, text-to-speech, and translation for health communication in local languages

- AI-supported health administration (insurance claims and fraud detection, supply-chain forecasting, record digitisation)

Exclude ordinary digital health tools where AI is not a substantive component (e.g., rule-based SMS reminders, electronic health records, telemedicine platforms without an AI function).

## **4. Type of evidence to prioritize**

Prioritize sources that contain information on **real interventions or real deployments**, including:

- randomized controlled trials

- field experiments

- quasi-experimental evaluations

- prospective interventional cohorts in routine care

- prospective diagnostic-accuracy studies in real screening or care settings

- implementation studies and human-centred field evaluations of deployed systems

- deployment evaluations and scaling studies

- observational studies using actual usage logs or programme records

- national programme and ministry of health evaluations

- WHO, Stop TB Partnership, World Bank, and other international-organization reports

- credible programme evaluations and implementation reports

- cost, cost-effectiveness, budget-impact, or financing reports based on actual deployments

Do not prioritize:

- opinion pieces and editorials

- purely conceptual papers

- general discussions or narrative reviews of AI in health (use them only to locate primary studies)

- attitude-only or acceptability-only studies

- intention-to-use surveys without actual usage data

- purely technical machine-learning papers without real clinical or programme deployment

- papers where AI is used only for generic convenience

### **Sources to search**

Prioritize the sources listed below. They are not the only sources to use. You should also search more widely in any other databases, journals, conference proceedings, working-paper series, repositories, organizational websites, or grey-literature sources you judge likely to contain relevant studies.

1.  **Academic databases:** Google Scholar, OpenAlex, FAO AGRIS, AgEcon Search; supplementary searches in CAB Abstracts, Scopus, Web of Science, and EconLit.

2.  **Agricultural research institutions:** CGIAR and its research centres, IFPRI, FAO, CABI, and relevant national agricultural research institutes.

3.  **Development organizations and implementation evidence:** World Bank, ADB, IFAD, GSMA AgriTech, Precision Development (PxD), Digital Green, implementing NGOs, and agricultural technology providers.

4.  **AI4SG and impact evaluation repositories:** AI4D, 3ie Development Evidence Portal, AEA RCT Registry, J-PAL, World Bank DIME, and relevant institutional evaluation repositories.

5.  **Regional and national sources:** GARUDA (Indonesia), ThaiJO (Thailand), BanglaJOL (Bangladesh), national agriculture ministries, agricultural extension agencies, government project reports, and relevant local-language publications.

6.  **Conference proceedings:** ICTD, ACM COMPASS, ACM CHI, ACM CSCW, ACM DEV, AAAI, IJCAI, ACM SIGKDD, NeurIPS, ICML, and relevant agricultural economics and engineering conferences.

7.  **Additional grey literature:** Government evaluations, project reports, donor publications, NGO reports, agricultural extension service reports, developer documentation, and credible news articles.

8.  **Citation tracking:** Check reference lists, citing articles, related publications, and reports associated with identified AI4SG applications. Use the names of relevant programs, developers, and implementing organizations to locate additional evidence.

For highly relevant programs, also search the **program or tool name directly** to locate additional studies or implementation reports.

Where possible, connect multiple sources referring to the same AI tool or program.

## **6. Unit of analysis**

Use the **AI intervention or deployment** as the main unit of analysis, rather than the paper alone.

If several papers evaluate the same AI tool in different settings or stages, treat them as separate intervention records but connect them using a common:

- Tool / program name

- Organization / provider

- Program ID if useful

## **7. Information to extract**

For every relevant intervention, extract only information that is directly useful for later analysis.

### **A. Source information**

- Full citation

- Authors

- Year

- Paper/report title

- Journal, conference (and track), or institution

- DOI

- Official URL

- Trial registration ID, if any

- Publication type

### **B. Context**

- Country

- State / province / district / city, if reported

- Country income level (World Bank classification for the study year)

- Urban / rural / mixed

- Public / private / NGO / mixed health setting

- Level of care (household or community; primary care, e.g., PHC, health and wellness centre, puskesmas, sub-district health-promoting hospital; secondary; tertiary; national programme)

- Health area / disease

- Target population

- Age group and sex, if relevant

- Number of patients or beneficiaries

- Number of health workers, by cadre (specialists, doctors, nurses, community health workers)

- Number of facilities or sites

- Other relevant sample characteristics

### **C. Health problem**

Record the main problem the AI tool is intended to address.

Examples:

- undiagnosed or late-detected disease

- shortage of specialists (e.g., radiologists, ophthalmologists)

- high workload of frontline health workers

- limited health-worker knowledge or training

- long travel distances and limited access to care

- low engagement or drop-out from a health programme

- poor treatment adherence

- maternal and neonatal risk

- variable diagnostic quality

- patient backlogs and long waiting times

- language and literacy barriers

- financial barriers to care

- weak disease surveillance

- inefficiency or fraud in health financing and administration

- unmet mental-health needs and limited access to mental-health care

Use the authors' description where possible.

### **D. AI application**

Extract:

- AI tool / program name and version

- Provider / developer

- Regulatory status, if reported

- AI application category

- Technology type (e.g., convolutional neural network, gradient-boosting risk model, LLM with retrieval)

- Main function of the AI

- Level of AI autonomy:

  - assistive (human makes final decision)

  - triage (AI decides who receives further human or laboratory assessment)

  - autonomous (AI output is the decision for some patients)

- Primary user:

  - patient / beneficiary / caregiver

  - community health worker

  - nurse or allied health worker

  - doctor or specialist

  - programme manager or administrator

  - policymaker

  - other

Application categories may include:

- screening / diagnosis

- triage / referral

- clinical decision support

- patient-facing information / chatbot

- health-worker information support

- risk prediction / early warning

- resource allocation / outreach targeting

- adherence / behaviour change

- surveillance / forecasting

- language / translation

- administration / insurance / supply chain

### **E. Intervention design**

Extract:

- What exactly was introduced?

- Who received the intervention?

- What did users do with the AI?

- What role did clinicians or health workers play (human-in-the-loop, over-reading, confirmatory testing)?

- Did AI:

  - replace a human reading or decision

  - triage cases before human assessment

  - support a clinician or health worker

  - provide information or advice directly to patients

  - make predictions or prioritise beneficiaries

  - support administration

- Care pathway after the AI output (e.g., referral, confirmatory test such as Xpert, treatment initiation)

- AI threshold or operating point used

- Reference standard

- Duration

- Frequency

- Session length

- Intensity

- Location of use

- Control/comparison condition, if applicable

### **F. Implementation**

Extract concrete information on:

- health-worker training

- staff training

- technical support

- monitoring and quality assurance

- supervision

- implementation staff

- implementation fidelity

- incentives

- clinician and health-worker involvement

- human-AI interaction (trust, over-reliance, overriding AI outputs)

- workflow integration (patient flow, waiting time, extra steps)

- image or data quality (e.g., ungradable images, lighting, device quality)

- equipment requirements (e.g., digital X-ray, fundus camera, mobile van)

- organizational requirements

- digital literacy

- technical difficulties

- resistance

- patient and clinician trust

- privacy, consent, and data-governance concerns

- data requirements

- internet problems

- electricity problems

- device constraints

- downstream service capacity (e.g., referral or treatment capacity after positive screens)

- regulatory or medico-legal barriers

- other implementation barriers

- implementation facilitators

- smartphone

- tablet

- laptop

- IVR / voice call

- SMS

- offline capability

- electricity requirement

### **H. Scaling**

Extract factual information on:

- initial pilot size

- later deployment size

- number of people screened or served

- number of health workers

- number of facilities

- number of districts

- number of states / provinces

- geographic expansion

- expansion over time

- whether government adopted the tool

- whether the tool was integrated into public health facilities or a national programme

- inclusion in national guidelines

- institutionalization

- procurement

- regulatory approval obtained for scale-up

- modifications made when scaling

- staffing changes

- infrastructure changes

- operational requirements at scale

Do not label something "scaled" only because the authors describe it as scalable. Record actual deployment numbers where possible.

### **I. Cost**

Extract:

- total program cost

- cost per person screened

- cost per case detected

- cost per patient

- cost per health worker

- cost per facility

- cost per user

- marginal cost

- fixed cost

- software or per-scan licence cost

- hardware/device cost

- staffing cost

- technical-support cost

- period over which cost is calculated

## **8. Missing information**

Do not guess.

If a field is unavailable, record:

**Not reported**

If information was searched for but could not be verified, record:

**Not verified**

Do not infer values from surrounding information.

19. Control / Comparison Condition

## **10. Final output requirements**

The main output should be a **clean, verified evidence database** that can later be used for cross-study analysis.

The goal is:

> **To collect comparable factual information on how AI health tools in Bangladesh, India, Indonesia, and Thailand are designed, adopted, implemented, deployed, used, scaled, funded, and associated with health and health-system outcomes.**
